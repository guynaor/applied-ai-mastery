Which one corner of your home annoys you daily, but never quite enough to fix?

Today we design one small fix — and test it in cardboard before spending anything.

Assumptions are not the problem. Assumptions that look like measurements are.

Slide 3 — The assumption everyone forgets

“The drawer opens fully”

Nobody measured that. Cheap runners stop at 80 per cent.

If that holds, the usable depth is 33.6 cm, not 42 — and the back of the organiser you designed is out of reach.

One measurement you did not take outranks every calculation you did.

Slide 4 — Constraints, minutes 17–29

Three clearances people forget

  • A finger: it has to get in to lift something out — at least 1.5 cm around each compartment.
  • Above: the organiser must not touch the runner — 0.5 cm.
  • The wall thickness itself: it eats into the internal dimension, not the external one.

Slide 5 — The request

The tool has not seen the room

These are measurements I took in a drawer, each with a source label. Turn them into a specification for a drawer organiser that carries no weight and involves no electrics. Separate clearly: measured values, assumptions, clearances, and one adjustable parameter. Flag every place where I am missing a dimension. Do not claim the design is safe or engineering-approved.

Slide 6 — The parameter you change

Predict first, then check

Number of cable compartments: from 5 to 7.

Write down before you change it: what should happen to the width of each one? Then change it, and check whether that is what happened.
If your prediction was wrong, you did not understand the model — and that is exactly what you want to find out now rather than after printing.

Slide 7 — The cardboard test, minutes 44–54

The cheapest test you will run

Cut a rectangle at the real size, pencil in the compartments, put it in place — and use it as you would on an ordinary day.

What to testWhy that specifically
That it fits the widthThe easiest dimension to get wrong
That something comes out one-handedThat is how a drawer is actually used
That the drawer still closesThe expensive failure: found after building

Slide 8 — What a drawing does not prove

A convincing sketch is not proof that something fits

A neat render, a clean 3D model, an image from the tool — all of them look right even when one dimension is wrong.

The cardboard is the only thing here that touches reality.

Slide 9 — The limit of this session

What we are not designing today

  • Nothing that carries a person's weight
  • Nothing involving electrics
  • Nothing that is dangerous if it fails
We are designing an organiser, not a shelf above a cot. That is not modesty — it is scoping.
