Slide 3 — The forgotten assumption

“The drawer opens fully”

Nobody measured that. Cheap runners stop at 80 per cent.

If that holds, usable depth is 33.6 cm, not 42 — and the back of the organiser is out of reach.

Slide 5 — The request, minutes 30–55

Give it real numbers

These are measurements I took, each with a source label: [the measurements]. I want a drawer organiser with compartments for cables. Build a parametric model where every dimension derives from the numbers I gave, with the parameters at the top of the file so I can change them. Do not invent dimensions — if something is missing, ask me instead of filling it in. Do not claim the design is strong or safe.

Slide 6 — The question that comes after

“Which dimensions did you use that I did not give you?”

It will produce a list: wall thickness, clearances, heights, fillet radii.

None of them is necessarily wrong. They simply were not measured — the same assumption-that-looks-like-a-measurement, now inside geometry.

Slide 8 — Physical check, minutes 70–85

The cardboard is the only thing that touches reality

Cut to the model's dimensions, put it in place, and use it normally.

What to testWhy that specifically
That it fits the widthThe easiest dimension to get wrong
That something comes out one-handedThat is how it is actually used
That the drawer still closesThe expensive failure: found after printing

Slide 9 — The limit

What we are not designing today

  • Nothing that carries a person's weight
  • Nothing involving electrics
  • Nothing that is dangerous if it fails
And the tool does not know this. It will happily build a bracket for a heavy shelf and tell you it looks good.
