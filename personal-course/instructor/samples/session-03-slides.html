Slide 1 — Opening, minutes 0–5

What are you organising right now that needs other people to happen?

Today we are not learning a planning method. We are doing the work itself with AI — food, drinks, an image display, timing, and dividing up — and after each job we ask the same question.

Slide 2 — The question that returns five times

After every output the tool gives you

  1. What did it assume that I never told it?
  2. What did it invent that sounds like a fact?
  3. Who owns this in practice?
Those three questions are the whole lesson. Everything else is practising them on real jobs.

Slide 3 — Food and drinks, minutes 5–15

Asking for quantities

We are 40 people at a four-hour evening event, including 3 vegetarians and 4 children. The food budget is 3,800. Give me quantities and a shopping list. At the end, separate clearly: what I told you, versus what you assumed yourself — portions per person, how much people drink, and anything else you filled in.
That last sentence is the whole difference. Without it you get numbers with no idea where they came from.

Slide 5 — Image display, minutes 15–28

Building something that will run at the event itself

Build one self-contained web page that shows images on a loop: each image for 8 seconds, a caption beneath it, a pause and resume control, and a way to change the speed. Everything in a single file, no login, and nothing sent anywhere. Use placeholder images for now that I can swap out later.

Slide 6 — Two checks and one question

Before trusting the display

The checkWhat happens
Only two imagesDoes the loop still work, or flicker?
A very long captionDoes the layout break? Cover the image?
And the question that arrives on its own: whose photos are these, and who agreed to appear in them?

Three conflicts are waiting between the sources. Let the tool arrange, then hunt for them yourself.

Slide 8 — Dividing the work, minutes 38–50

“Someone should” is not a task

What the tool writesWhat you fix it to
“Invitations need sending”“Aunt sends them, by 1 October”
“Confirm numbers with the caterer”“Partner, by 28 October”
“Sort out music”“My brother, with a backup playlist on my phone”
And what the tool cannot know: who is genuinely free, and who has already taken on too much.

Slide 9 — What breaks, minutes 50–60

One card, and a fallback with three things

What happens instead — one clear action.

Who triggers it — a name, not “someone”.

By when it must be decided — the moment after which there is no choice.

“We'll see what happens” postpones the decision to the moment it costs more.

Slide 10 — Exit check

Complete out loud or in writing

The tool saved me [what]. Its assumption that surprised me most was [what]. The task that now has a name is [what], and it belongs to [who] by [when].

And the reflection question

What did the tool do better than you — and what did you do better than it?
