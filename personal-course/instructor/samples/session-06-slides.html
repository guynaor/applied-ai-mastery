Slide 1 — Opening, minutes 0–5

When did you last see an image online and believe it immediately?

Today we build one visual story you can stand behind. Not a dull one — one whose claims hold up when someone checks them.

Slide 2 — The trust boundary, minutes 5–13

Four decisions before touching an image

The decisionExample
Who am I tellingNeighbours in the building's group chat
What is it forSo they join the next volunteering day
Which claims can I support“We cleared the bed in one day” — I have before and after
Who agreed to appearTwo of three. The third was never asked.

Slide 3 — Sorting the evidence, minutes 13–23

Three labels for every asset

Original — I took it, and changed nothing beyond a crop.

Edited — I took it, but changed something that changes the meaning.

Generated — it does not exist in reality.

The label alone is not enough. The real question is what a viewer takes from the image together with the caption beside it.

Slide 4 — The moment that matters

An unlabelled generated image is not decoration

An AI-generated image, captioned “this is how the garden looks now”.

That is a factual claim about a real place, supported by an image that is not a record.

Either label it clearly as an illustration and change the caption, or take it out. There is no third option.

Slide 5 — The harder problem to see

A completely real photo, in the wrong context

A genuine photo of an abundant bed — from another garden, last July — captioned “this is what we grew”.

Not a pixel here was invented. The deception is entirely in the caption.

Slide 6 — The request, minutes 23–38

Build a storyboard, not content

From these notes about the volunteering day, build a six-panel storyboard: a short caption and alt text for each panel. Separately flag which claims need a source I have to attach. Do not invent quotes, numbers, or details I did not give you, and do not suggest presenting a generated image as if it were a record.

Slide 7 — Through a viewer's eyes, minutes 38–50

Four checks, a minute each

The checkWhat to look for
Alt textA sentence describing what is visible — not “a photo of the garden”
ContrastWhite text over a bright image almost always fails
Not colour alone“Before” and “after” need a word too, not just a hue
Read it aloudRead the story from the alt text alone. Does it still make sense?

Slide 8 — The question that settles it

“Could a reasonable viewer take something untrue from this?”

Not “did I lie”. People almost never lie in a story like this — they simply do not write down what was obvious to them.

Slide 9 — Consent

“It was a public place” is not consent

  • An identifiable person in shot — ask them.
  • Children — ask the parents, always.
  • Anyone who said no, or was never asked — blur, crop, or leave them out.
And “a public image I found online” is not permission to use it either.

Slide 10 — Exit check, minutes 50–60

Complete out loud or in writing

My story has [number] assets. [how many] are edited or generated, and I labelled them by [how]. The claim that needed a source is [what], and the source is [what]. Before publishing I still need [what].

And the reflection question

Which image did you most want to use — and could not?
