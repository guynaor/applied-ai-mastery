Slide 1 — The three rules, minutes 10–16

Three rules, stated once, applied all session

Label it. Anything generated gets said out loud — in the caption, not buried in a settings screen.

No real people without consent. Not even from a public place.

Never a record. Generated work does not stand in for something that actually happened.

Six stages from here. At every one of them, ask these three questions again — that repetition is the safety lesson, not a slide you see once and forget.

Slide 3 — Prompt anatomy, minutes 16–27

Five things a prompt has to say

  • Subject — what is actually in the frame
  • Composition — the angle and distance it's seen from
  • Light — the light source, direction, and time of day
  • Framing — where the empty space sits, for a title later
  • Exclusions — what must not appear
Weak
“A nice photo of a garden.”
Strong
“A community garden bed in full bloom, seen at eye level from the path. Golden late-afternoon light from the left casting long shadows. Leave the upper-left quarter empty for a title. No people, no signage, no litter.”

Same intent, five specifics added. Leave one out and the model guesses — and the guess is rarely the one you wanted.

Slide 5 — Fix the region, minutes 27–38

Fix the region, do not re-roll

Re-rolling the whole prompt destroys whatever already worked, along with whatever was wrong.

One region is off — a hand, a sign, a shadow. Edit only that region. Everything else stays exactly as it was.

Same tool as stage 1. Aim the edit at the patch that failed, not at the picture again from nothing.

Slide 6 — Animate your own image, minutes 38–46

The prompt steers the motion. It does not invent the subject.

Upload the image from stages 1 and 2. Add a short prompt describing what moves, and how.

“Leaves sway gently in the light breeze. Camera holds still. Nothing else in frame changes.”
This is still your image. The model is moving it, not replacing it.

Slide 7 — Generate from text alone, minutes 46–56

Text, hands, continuity, physics — where video generation still breaks

  • Text — signage and labels come out wrong more often than right
  • Hands — count the fingers before you trust the frame
  • Continuity — what's true in frame one may not hold by frame four
  • Physics — weight, water, and shadows drift
Shoot short. A four-second clip that holds together beats a fifteen-second clip that falls apart halfway through. Send the job, then keep talking — it is not instant.

Slide 8 — The safety callback, minutes 46–56

Which of those two would you publish about a real place?

Both clips are generated — nothing here was shot with an actual camera. The real difference is how much you directed: stage 3 animates an image you built and fixed; stage 4 came from a text prompt alone, with nothing of yours in it.

If you captioned either one “this is this place,” right now — would that be true?

Neither one — not without a label. That's rule three from slide 1. Say so out loud, before anyone asks; that's rule one, applied to something you made ten minutes ago.

Slide 9 — Write for the ear, minutes 56–65

Write for the ear, not the eye

Narrate the piece you've built — your subject, the image, and the two clips.

  • Sentences — short. If it needs a comma to survive, split it.
  • One idea — per breath. One clause per sentence, not three joined with “and.”
  • Numbers — spoken, not written: “about twelve thousand people,” not “12,847 attendees.”
The script matters more than the voice. Fix it on the page before you generate the narration.
