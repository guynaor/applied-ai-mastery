# Full session plan — Session 7: Build a Personal System

**Time:** 90 minutes
**Learning outcome:** Learners gather real evidence of what they did, build a starting page they will return to, read a permission request before approving it, and set a stop line on what a tool may do on their behalf.
**Session artifact:** One starting page: goal, evidence record, portfolio note, finish plan, permission map, next check-in, and stop rule.

**This session runs 90 minutes, not 60.** The extra time is not overrun — it is questions, and that is where the learning happens.

This is the last session of the course, and it closes what Session 1 opened: who decides. No learner is required to connect a tool to their own account.

## The recommended way: a real case, on your screen

What works best is to **take a real case of your own and work it live in front of the class on your own screen.** Three reasons: learners' free accounts run out midway; a real case produces real questions; and they watch you correct the tool, which is worth more than any explanation.

Learners fill in the journal tables for **their own case**. You run it; they think.

**Before every prompt** ask: "What do you think it will come back with?" and "What is missing from my request?"
**When the answer returns**, read the first number aloud and ask "where is that from?", hunt for the assumption, and correct one thing in front of them.
**After each stage**, give two minutes for them to fill in the journal. A session where only you typed is a demonstration, not a lesson.

The prepared sample materials stay as backup — for a class with no network, when you have no example of your own, or for a facilitator who wants a guaranteed script.

## Start slow things in the background

Some requests take time — a detailed spreadsheet, a presentation, or anything that generates code. **Do not wait for them in front of the class.** Send it, keep talking about the next thing, and come back when it is ready.

## Before learners arrive

### Prepare the room and tools

1. If you are demonstrating **Claude Desktop**, prepare a sample folder of invented files in advance — like the one in the [permission map](../../instructor/samples/session-07-permission-map.html). Do not demonstrate on a real folder of your own.
2. If you are not demonstrating, the [permission map](../../instructor/samples/session-07-permission-map.html) contains the permission request and the decision table. The session works completely without installing anything.
3. Open the [slides](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html).
4. Bring the group's artifacts from earlier sessions if you kept them. They are today's evidence.

### What learners need

- Three to five things they actually did, in the course or outside it.
- The journal, or paper.
- A device is entirely optional in this session.

### Two facilitator decisions before starting

**Whether to demonstrate a real connection.** The demonstration is powerful, but it is not necessary. If there is any doubt about what is in the folder, do not demonstrate. The permission map teaches the same thing with no risk.

**How much time to give the portfolio.** Learners tend to want to rewrite everything they did. Stop that: three honest lines are worth more than a polished page, and they are what the next employer or teacher actually reads.

## Session map

| Time | What the facilitator does | What learners produce or identify |

## 0–10 minutes — Homework discussion

**Start here in every session from the second onwards.** It works well, and not because it is a check — because a learner who hears what broke for someone else learns faster than from a lesson.

Three questions, in this order:

1. **"Who managed to do something with what we talked about?"** Two or three tell it.
2. **"Who did it not work for, and where did it get stuck?"** This is the important one. If nobody answers, tell them about something that got stuck for you.
3. **"What surprised you?"** The best insights arrive here.

Note two things that came up, and return to them later in the session by the name of whoever raised them.

## 10–15 minutes — Opening

Show [slide 1](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html): “Of everything we did in this course, what will you actually keep doing?”

### What to say

> “This is the last session, so we are closing what we opened in the first one: who decides. Today we build one starting page, and its only measure is whether you come back to it in a month.”

## 15–30 minutes — Real evidence

Show [slide 2](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html). Each learner records three to five things: what happened, where it is recorded, what they did themselves, and what they still do not know.

### What to say

> “Do not invent results — and do not quietly improve them either. ‘I tried it and it did not work’ is evidence exactly as good as ‘it worked’, and sometimes it teaches more. The fourth column, ‘what I still do not know’, is what separates a portfolio from an advert.”

## 30–45 minutes — The starting page

Show [slide 3](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html) and the four lines: the next step, the check-in cadence, where things live, and what “done” looks like.

The fourth is the one that gets forgotten. A goal with no finish condition runs forever and is then abandoned.

## 45–65 minutes — Permissions

Show [slide 4](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html) and the permission request from the [permission map](../../instructor/samples/session-07-permission-map.html). Read it aloud, line by line, before touching a button.

### The moment that matters: which button is prominent

Show [slide 5](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html). “Choose a specific folder” is the quiet button. “Allow” is the coloured one.

### What to say

> “That is not malice. It is a default that suits whoever built the interface. But that is exactly why the decision has to be yours rather than the design's — everywhere you meet a permission request, not only here.”

Work through the decision table on [slide 6](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html): what to approve, what not to, and why. Then, if you are demonstrating, run the request against the sample folder and verify afterwards using [slide 7](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html).

### If the demonstration fails

That is entirely fine, and it is an opportunity. “The tool could not read the folder” is precisely the result that shows the permission was narrow. Do not fix it by widening access in front of the class.

## 65–80 minutes — The stop line and the run record

Show [slide 8](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html): what is off limits, what always needs approval, and when it stops.

Anyone who did the OpenClaw lab in Session 2 now reviews their **agent run record** against [slide 9](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html). The sample record is in the [permission map](../../instructor/samples/session-07-permission-map.html), and one field is deliberately missing: the **human approval point**. Let the class find it.

### What to say

> “A run that worked is evidence to review, not permission to widen access. That may be the most important sentence in the whole course, and it holds for tools that do not exist yet.”

## 80–90 minutes — Closing the course

Show [slide 10](../../instructor/samples/session-07-slides.html):

> I will keep working on **[what]**. The next step is **[what]**, by **[when]**. The only access I granted is **[what]**, and my approval will always be required before **[what]**.

The reflection question that closes the course: “What did this course change about how you decide — not about how you type?”

### Quick assessment rubric

| Evidence | Not yet | Ready |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence | A description of what they meant to do | Things that happened, with somewhere they are recorded |
| Honesty | Only what succeeded | Also what failed, and what is still unknown |
| Permissions | “I approved it so it would work” | Least privilege, and verification after the run |
| Stopping | “I'll keep an eye on it” | A written condition after which the system halts |

## Equivalent tool routes

**No tool (a full route):** The permission map on paper, and the evidence record by hand. There is no learning gap here.
**Claude Desktop:** Only on an invented sample folder, in the facilitator account, read-only.
**ChatGPT / Gemini:** Paste the text of invented lists and run the same request.
**Claude for Chrome:** Further exploration only, after the course. Start read-only.

## Troubleshoot without losing the lesson

| Situation | Facilitator response |
|---|---|
| No safe sample folder | Use the permission map alone. The session is complete. |
| The demonstration failed | Excellent. Show that this is correct behaviour from a narrow permission. |
| A learner wants to connect their own account | Not today. Their permissions are not demonstration material. |
| “I don't have enough evidence” | They do. “I tried and did not manage it” is evidence, often the best kind. |
| The portfolio drifts into rewriting everything | Three honest lines. Stop there. |
| Time runs out | Drop the live demonstration, not the stop line. |

## Differentiation

- **No device:** The whole session works on paper, including the permission map.
- **For learners who need structure:** Give the four starting-page lines as a template to fill in.
- **For learners ready to extend:** Ask them to write the stop rule for a system they genuinely use today.
- **Anyone who missed sessions:** Evidence from outside the course is entirely valid.

## After the session

This is the end of the course, so it is worth asking what remains. If you can, go back to learners in a month with one question: “Did you return to your starting page?” The answer will teach you more about the course than any feedback you collect on the last day.
