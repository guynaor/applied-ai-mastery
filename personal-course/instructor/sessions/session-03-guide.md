# Full session plan — Session 3: Make a Shared Plan Work

**Time:** 90 minutes
**Learning outcome:** Learners carry out five real event-organising jobs with AI, and after each one identify what the tool assumed, what it invented, and who actually owns it.
**Session artifact:** One page you could send to whoever is helping: food and drink quantities with assumptions marked, a link to a running image display, a run sheet with a source for every changing fact, a task table with names and dates, and one fallback.

**This session runs 90 minutes, not 60.** The extra time is not overrun — it is questions. When you show people a tool doing something they have done by hand for years, they stop and ask, and that is where the learning happens. The questions are built into the map below.

## The recommended way: a real event, on your screen

What works best is to **take a real event you are organising and work it live in front of the class on your own screen.** Not because it is easier, but for three reasons:

- **Free accounts run out.** Most learners have a small daily quota. If everyone runs five jobs, some will stall halfway. When the work runs on your account, nobody gets blocked.
- **A real case produces real questions.** "Wait, how did it know how much people drink?" only gets asked about an event that is actually going to happen.
- **They watch you correct it.** When the tool returns something wrong and you argue with it on screen, that is worth more than any explanation.

**This session is demonstrated, not worked through.** With twenty or more people on a video call, individual hands-on work does not run — people fall behind silently, and you cannot see it. You do every step on your own screen, and the journal tables are the homework, filled in for their own event during the week.

That does not mean they sit quietly. The three questions on slide 2 are put to the room after every output, and those answers are the lesson. When you reach a judgement call, stop and ask before you decide — let two or three people answer, including a wrong one.

### What to bring

One event you are genuinely organising that has numbers in it: how many people, budget, date, venue. A family event, a class evening, a team day, an end-of-year party. If you have none, take the [planning sources](../../instructor/samples/session-03-sources.html) and work them exactly the same way.

### What to ask before every prompt

Before you type, ask the class — this is what turns a demonstration into a lesson:

- "What do you think it will come back with?" Collect one or two guesses before it answers.
- "What does it need to know that I have not told it?" They will find what you forgot.
- "What am I not willing to give it?" Names, phone numbers, the exact address.

### What to highlight when the answer comes back

- **Read the first number it gave you out loud** and ask "where is that from?"
- **Hunt for the assumption** before celebrating the output. There is always one.
- **Correct one thing in front of them** and re-run. They see this is a process, not magic.
- **Say it out loud when it got something right.** We are not teaching scepticism, we are teaching checking, and those are not the same.

### When to stop and hand it back

After each job, two minutes where they fill in the journal for their own case. Do not skip it because "we're mid-flow" — a session where only you typed is a demonstration, not a lesson.

## Start slow things in the background

Some requests take time — a detailed table, a planning spreadsheet, a presentation, or a page that generates code. **Do not wait for them in front of the class.** Send them, and move on to the next thing while they run.

In this session: at minute 15, before the food discussion, send the **image display** request. It will build while you talk about quantities, and be ready when you reach it at minute 30.

The same rule applies to anything taking more than half a minute: send, keep talking, come back.

## Before learners arrive

1. Open the [slides](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) for screen sharing, and the tool you will work in — **Claude** is convenient here because of Artifacts.
2. Prepare **one shared document** everyone writes in. Five headings: food and drinks, image display, timing, dividing work, what breaks.
3. Have your real event's details in a file ready to paste — without full names or phone numbers.
4. Have the [disruption cards](../../instructor/samples/session-03-cards.html) link ready for the chat. Do not send it early.

### What learners need

- One real event of their own to fill the journal in against.
- A device. An active AI tool is not required — the heavy work runs on your account.
- Access to the shared document.

## 0–10 minutes — Homework discussion

**Start here in every session from the second onwards.** It works well, and not because it is a check — because a learner who hears what broke for someone else learns faster than from a lesson.

**This one is specifically the agent.** Session 2's homework was to run it for the week and bring the run record, so start there rather than with the general question:

1. **"What did your agent catch, and what did it miss?"** The misses are the useful half.
2. **"Did anything fire twice about the same thing?"** Duplicate suppression is the part that always looks right on paper and fails in the world.
3. **"Would you give it more access than you gave it last week — and why?"** Sit with the answer. A learner who says yes has usually found a real limit; a learner who says no has usually been burned by something.

**If someone's agent never fired**, that is a finding and not a failure. Ask which it was: a trigger that was wrong, or a week in which genuinely nothing happened. Those are different problems and only one of them is fixable.

Then the general questions, briefly: what worked for someone else, and what surprised them.

Write two things on screen that came up. If something is relevant to today, return to it later by the name of whoever raised it.

### What to say

> "Before we start — what happened for you since? Especially what did not work. I learn more from that than you do."

## 10–15 minutes — Opening

Show [slide 1](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) and your real event: "This is what I am organising, and these are the numbers."

Then [slide 2](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) — the three questions that return five times: what did it **assume**, what did it **invent**, and who **owns** it.

## 15–30 minutes — Food and drinks

**First, send the image display request** ([slide 5](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html)) and leave it building in another tab. You now have 15 minutes while it works.

Show the request on [slide 3](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) with your event's real numbers. Before sending, ask: "What do you think it will come back with?"

Note the last sentence of the request: **"separate what I told you from what you assumed yourself."**

### The moment that matters

Show [slide 4](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) and compare with the assumptions it actually made. One and a half portions per person, two drinks an hour, children eating like adults, guests staying to the end.

### What to say

> "None of those assumptions is wrong. They are simply not mine. The gap between one and one and a half portions is real money, and it was decided by something with no idea how much people eat at my events."

**Two minutes in the journal** — everyone fills in table 1 for their own event.

## 30–50 minutes — The image display

The request running in the background is ready now. This is the long block, and the only one producing something that will run at the event itself.

### The two checks

Show [slide 6](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html): what happens with **only two images**, and with **a very long caption**.

### Getting it out of the tool — this is not obvious

The artifact runs inside Claude's preview pane, but **to run it at the real event you need it as a file of your own**. Show this in front of the class, because it is exactly where people get stuck alone:

1. In the artifact's corner there is a **download** option, or copy the code. Download it, or paste it into a file and save as `display.html`.
2. Open the file in a browser by double-clicking. It should run with no internet.
3. **If the images do not appear** — this is the common failure. The images it used are external links or placeholders, and they did not come down with the file.

To fix it, ask the tool exactly this:

> The file runs locally but the images do not appear. Change it so it loads images from a local folder called `images` sitting next to the file, named `1.jpg`, `2.jpg` and so on. If a file is missing, skip it without breaking the loop. Do not use any internet address.

### What to say

> "Notice what happened. The tool built something that works for it and does not work for me. That is not a bug — it is the difference between showing and handing over. Whenever someone builds you something, always ask where it will run in the end."

### And the question that arrives on its own

**Whose photos are these, and who agreed to appear in them.**

**Two minutes in the journal.**

## 50–65 minutes — Timing

Give the tool your event's details and ask for a run sheet. If you are working from the [planning sources](../../instructor/samples/session-03-sources.html), give it the page address or the text from the copy button.

Show [slide 7](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html): which facts change, and a source and date for each.

**If you are on a real event:** the strong question here is "when did I last check this?" about the venue's hours and the supplier's terms. The answer is usually "when I booked", and that was a while ago.

**If you are on the sample sources:** three conflicts are waiting — the hall until 22:00 against a plan to 23:00, the last bus at 22:40, and the caterer closing numbers 10 days out against a printer needing 7 working days.

**Two minutes in the journal.**

## 65–80 minutes — Dividing the work

Ask for a split with owners and dates, and show [slide 8](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html).

If someone asks for a **proper spreadsheet** for this — a good request, and a slow one. Send it now and keep talking while it builds.

The tool will write "someone should send invitations". That is the line to fix: **a name and a date.**

### What to say

> "What the tool cannot know: who is genuinely free this week, and who has already taken on too much. That is not a gap in the tool — that is your part."

**Three minutes in the journal** — this is the table they take away.

## 80–90 minutes — What breaks, and closing

**Now** open the [disruption cards](../../instructor/samples/session-03-cards.html) on screen. Read two out and run *your* plan against them live, thinking aloud: what breaks, what we do instead, who calls it. Ask the room for the fallback before you give yours.

Then send the card set to the chat. Running their own plan against a card is the homework, and it is the part that makes the fallback real.

[Slide 9](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html): a fallback needs what happens instead, who triggers it, and by when.

Then [slide 10](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) for the exit check:

> The tool saved me **[what]**. Its assumption that surprised me most was **[what]**. The task that now has a name is **[what]**, and it belongs to **[who]** by **[when]**.

### The homework — say it out loud

"Before next session, run at least two of the jobs on your own event. **I will ask at the start what did not work**, so notice where you get stuck."

### Quick assessment rubric

| Evidence | Not yet | Ready |
|---|---|---|
| Assumptions | They got quantities | Assumptions listed separately from facts |
| The display | "It looks fine" | Downloaded, ran locally, tested with two images and a long caption |
| Changing facts | "The venue is open late" | A source and date for every fact that changes |
| Ownership | "Invitations need sending" | "Aunt, by 1 October" |
| Fallback | "We'll see what happens" | What, who, and by when |

## Equivalent tool routes

**Claude (recommended):** Artifacts for the image display, and the same requests for the rest.
**ChatGPT / Gemini:** The same requests. For the display, ask for one HTML file and download it the same way.
**A tool that cannot open links:** The copy button at the top of the sources page.
**Learners with no tool:** That is the default here. You run it, they fill in.

## Troubleshoot without losing the lesson

| Situation | Facilitator response |
|---|---|
| Images missing in the downloaded file | The prompt in the download section. This is a lesson, not a fault. |
| Someone's free quota runs out | Which is why we run on your screen. They fill in the journal and carry on. |
| The tool invented times or prices | Stop and point at it. Move it to "assumption". |
| Questions overrun | Good. That is why this session is 90 minutes. If a block is squeezed, shorten dividing the work. |
| Someone opened the disruption cards early | Give them a different card. |
| You have no real event | The planning sources, run exactly the same way. |
| Time runs out | Drop dividing the work. Not the disruption card, and not the download. |

## Differentiation

- **Remote:** The session was designed this way. One shared screen, one shared document.
- **No device:** Fill in the journal on paper. No gap.
- **Anyone who wants to run it themselves:** Good, but warn them the quota may run out. Have them pick one job.
- **For learners ready to extend:** Make the display work from a phone too, and actually test that.

## After the session

Note which of the tool's assumptions most surprised the group, and which question came up more than once — a repeated question belongs in the slides. Keep the two things raised in the homework discussion, to open the next session with.
