# Full session plan — Session 3: Make a Shared Plan Work

**Time:** 90 minutes
**Learning outcome:** Learners watch one real event — one they chose themselves — become three files you could hand to somebody else, and after each file identify what the tool assumed, what it invented, and who actually owns it.
**Session artifact:** Three files on screen: an **Excel sheet** for planning, a **Word document** with the run sheet and operating instructions, and an **HTML page** that plays music and shows photos on the night.

**This session runs 90 minutes, not 60.** The extra time is not overrun — it is questions. When you show people a tool doing something they have done by hand for years, they stop and ask, and that is where the learning happens. The questions are built into the map below.

## What changed in this session

Until now the session ran on an event of yours, or on a prepared sources page. From now it runs on **an event the room picks live**, and produces **three real files** instead of five chat answers.

That is one change with two consequences. First, they watch *their own* event go into the tool, which produces questions an invented event never produces. Second, at the end there are three files you can open rather than a transcript of a conversation — and the difference between "the tool answered nicely" and "I have a file I can send somebody" is the whole lesson.

## The way it runs: an event the room picks, on your screen

**This session is demonstrated, not worked through.** With twenty or more people on a video call, individual hands-on work does not run — people fall behind silently, and you cannot see it. You do every step on your own screen, and the journal tables are the homework.

What is new is where the event comes from. **Slide 1 asks the room what they are organising, and you pick one and work it.** Why that beats an event of yours:

- **Somebody in the room owning the case produces real questions.** "Wait, how did it know how much people drink?" lands differently when it is the asker's sister's wedding.
- **The owner is in the room.** When the tool makes a wrong assumption, somebody says "that is not true for me" — and there is no better demonstration than that.
- **They watch you correct it.** When the tool returns something wrong and you argue with it on screen, that is worth more than any explanation.

That does not mean they sit quietly. The three questions on slide 2 are put to the room after every file, and those answers are the lesson. When you reach a judgement call, stop and ask before you decide — let two or three people answer, including a wrong one.

### The one risk, and how you cover it

The live pick is the most alive part of the session and the least predictable. Two things can go wrong, and both have a thirty-second answer.

**If the chosen event is too thin** — "a get-together with some friends", no numbers, no constraints — do not swap it. **Keep the event and ask the room for the missing numbers:** roughly how many people, what budget, how many hours, where. If there is still not enough, borrow the numbers from the [planning sources](../../instructor/samples/session-03-sources.html) and say so out loud: "I am borrowing numbers so we have something to work with."

**If the event is too private** — a funeral, a divorce, something medical — thank whoever offered it, say "we will not do that one on a shared screen", and take the next suggestion. Do not deliberate out loud.

### Privacy — say this before the pick

This is a real person's real event, projected to twenty or more people. Before you ask for suggestions, say this:

> "We are working on somebody's actual event here, so: roles instead of surnames, no exact addresses, no phone numbers, no guest list. 'The bride's mother' works fine."

This is not only manners. It is exactly the "what am I not willing to give it" lesson, except this time it is enforced live in front of the room.

## What the three files are, and why these

| The file | What is in it | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| **Excel** | Food and drink quantities, a shopping list with prices, and a task sheet with an owner and a date | Assumptions become a column you can point at |
| **Word** | An hour-by-hour run sheet, operating instructions for each step, and a table of facts that can change | The difference between a plan and a plan you can hand over |
| **HTML** | Photos on a loop with background music, running four hours untouched | "Works for the tool" is not "works for me" |

The order is not arbitrary. **The sheet goes first** because the assumption hunt is sharpest there, and its numbers feed the other two. **The HTML goes last** because it is the only one you can send off to build in the background at minute 16 and come back to finished, and it is the ending that stays with them.

### How the journal maps on

**The journal has not changed, and does not need to.** Its five tables spread across the three files exactly:

- Tables 1 and 4 (quantities and work split) → **the sheet**
- Table 3 (run sheet and what changes) → **the Word document**
- Table 2 (the two tests) → **the HTML page**
- Table 5 → the fallback at the end

If anyone asks why the journal says "the image display" when we built a display with music — it is the same table, and both of its tests apply exactly as written.

## What to ask before every prompt

Before you type, ask the class — this is what turns a demonstration into a lesson:

- "What do you think it will come back with?" Collect one or two guesses before it answers.
- "What does it need to know that I have not told it?" They will find what you forgot.
- "What am I not willing to give it?" Full names, phone numbers, the exact address.

## What to draw attention to when the file comes back

- **Open the file.** Do not settle for the summary the tool wrote about it. Half the lesson is the difference between the two.
- **Read the first number you see out loud** and ask "where is that from?"
- **Hunt for the assumption** before you celebrate. There is always one.
- **Correct one thing in front of the class** and run it again. They see this is a process, not magic.
- **Say so out loud when it was right.** Scepticism is not what we teach — checking is, and they are not the same.

## Starting things in the background

Producing a file takes the tool longer than a chat answer does. **Do not wait in front of the class.** Send it, and move on to the next thing while it runs.

In this session: **at minute 16, right after the event is picked, send the HTML page request.** It builds while you work on the sheet and the document, and it is ready when you get to it at minute 58.

The same rule applies to anything that takes more than half a minute: send it, keep talking, come back.

## Before learners arrive

1. Open the [slides](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) for screen sharing, and **Claude with file creation turned on** — that is what produces real Excel and Word files.
2. **Test the layout tonight, not tomorrow.** Ask the tool for a small table in a Word file and an Excel file, open both, and see how they come out on your machine. See "Three traps" below — you want to know the behaviour in advance, not discover it in front of twenty people.
3. Prepare **a folder on your desktop** with an `images` subfolder holding three or four pictures named `1.jpg`, `2.jpg`, `3.jpg`, and a `music` subfolder with one or two tracks **named `1.mp3`, `2.mp3`** — the same numbering as the images. Without it the HTML block cannot be demonstrated.
4. Prepare **one shared document** everyone writes in — the event name and its numbers get written there live.
5. Have the [disruption cards](../../instructor/samples/session-03-cards.html) link ready to paste into chat. Do not send it early.
6. Open the [planning sources](../../instructor/samples/session-03-sources.html) in a tab — your backup if the room's pick is thin.

### What learners need

- One real event of their own to fill the journal in on — not necessarily the one that gets picked.
- A device. No active AI tool required — the heavy work runs on your machine.
- Access to the shared document.

## 0–10 minutes — homework discussion

**Start here in every session from the second onward.** This works very well, and not because of accountability — a learner who hears where somebody else got stuck learns faster than from a lesson.

**This time it is specifically the agent.** The homework from session 2 was to run it across the week and bring the run log, so start there rather than with the general question:

1. **"What did your agent catch, and what did it miss?"** The misses are the interesting half.
2. **"Did anything alert twice for the same thing?"** Deduplication is the part that always looks right on paper and breaks in reality.
3. **"Would you give it more access than you did a week ago — and why?"** Give the answer room. People who say yes have usually hit a real limit; people who say no have usually been burned by something.

**If somebody's agent never alerted at all** — that is a finding, not a failure. Ask which of the two it is: a wrong trigger, or a week where genuinely nothing happened. Those are different problems and only one is fixable.

Write two things that come up on screen. If something is relevant to today's session, come back to it later by the name of whoever raised it.

### What to say

> "Before we start — what happened for you since? Especially what did not work. I learn more from that than you do."

## 10–18 minutes — opening and picking the event

This is the new block, and it sets the quality of everything after it. Give it the full eight minutes.

**Privacy first.** Say the sentence from the section above, before anybody types anything.

Show [slide 1](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html): "What are you organising right now that needs other people to happen?" Ask for suggestions **in the chat**, not out loud — out loud you get two, in chat you get ten.

Give it a minute. Then pick one out loud and give a reason: "Let's take Dana's birthday — it has numbers, it has a venue, and it has somebody who will tell me when I am wrong."

### Getting the numbers out of the room

Now ask the event's owner four questions, and write the answers into the shared document in front of everyone:

1. **Roughly how many people?** "Roughly" is a good answer. It goes into the sheet too.
2. **When, and how many hours?**
3. **Where, and what time do you have to be out?**
4. **What is the budget, and what has to happen there?**

This looks like admin. It is not — **it is the session's first lesson.** Say it out loud:

> "Notice what we just did. Four questions, three minutes, and we have more context than most people ever give a tool. Most of the time AI comes back generic, it is because it got less than that."

### Then send the HTML off to the background

Show [slide 3](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) — the three files we are building today — then [slide 9](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html), the display page request. Send it **now** with the chosen event's details, and leave it running in another tab.

Say why: "I am sending this now because it will take a few minutes. There is no reason for us to sit and watch it work."

Then [slide 2](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) — the three questions that come back after every file: what did it **assume**, what did it **invent**, and who **owns** it.

## 18–40 minutes — the Excel sheet

The first long block. It swallows two jobs that used to be separate — quantities and the work split — because in a spreadsheet they are two tabs of the same file anyway.

Show [slide 4](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) with the real numbers you collected. Before you send it, ask: "What do you think it will come back with?"

Note the two last lines of the request: **an "assumption" column it is not allowed to leave empty**, and **everything in the right layout for the language**. The second is not cosmetic — see the first trap below.

### The moment that matters

When the file is ready — **open it.** Do not read out the summary the tool wrote in chat. Show the sheet.

Go to the assumptions column and read it out loud, row by row. Show [slide 5](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) and compare: a portion and a half per person, two cups an hour, children eat like adults, everybody stays to the end.

**Then turn to the event's owner and ask: "Is that true for you?"** This is the moment we picked a room event for.

### What to say

> "None of those assumptions is wrong. They are just not theirs. The gap between one portion and one and a half is real money, and it was decided by something that did not know how much people eat at their events."

### The second tab — the tasks

Switch to the task tab and show [slide 6](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html).

The tool will write "somebody needs to send invitations". **That is the line you fix, in front of the class: a name and a date.** Ask the owner who it actually is, and type it into the cell.

> "What the tool cannot know: who is actually free this week, and who has already taken on too much. That is not a gap in the tool — that is your part."

**Three minutes in the journal** — tables 1 and 4, everyone on their own event.

## 40–58 minutes — the Word document

Now you turn the sheet into something you can hand over. Show [slide 7](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html).

The distinction you are teaching here is between **a plan** and **a plan you can hand over**. Ask the room before you send it:

> "Who has organised something and held the whole thing in their head? And what happened when you went down with flu two days before?"

The request produces three parts: an hour-by-hour run sheet, short operating instructions for each step, and the table of facts that can change.

### The moment that matters

**The second part is the test.** Read one instruction out loud and ask the room: "If that line is all you get, do you know what to do?" Usually the answer is no, and that is exactly the place to fix it in front of the class.

**The third part is the sources lesson.** Show [slide 8](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html): three facts have a source and a date, and one has neither.

The strong question here is **"when did I last check that?"** about the venue's hours and the supplier's terms. Usually the answer is "when I booked", and that was a long time ago.

**If the tool invented a source** — and it might — stop and point at it. That is the most valuable finding in the session. "It wrote 'according to the venue's website'. It has not seen any website. That looks exactly like a real source."

**Two minutes in the journal** — table 3.

## 58–78 minutes — the music and photos page

The request you sent to the background is ready now. This is the only block that produces something that runs at the event itself, and it is also the one that almost always breaks the first time — **which is its value.**

### First run it where the tool is

Show that it works in the preview pane. Give the room a second to enjoy it. Then say: "Now let's try running it on my machine."

### Getting it out of the tool

1. In the corner of the artifact there is a **download** option, or copy the code. Download it, or paste it into a file and save it as `display.html` — **inside the folder you prepared**, next to `images` and `music`.
2. Open the file in a browser by double-clicking it.
3. Now you will hit one of the two traps, and possibly both.

### The two traps, and why they are the lesson

Show [slide 10](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) after you have seen what actually happened.

**Trap one: the images do not appear.** The tool used external links or placeholders, and they did not come down with the file.

**Trap two: the music does not play.** This is not a bug in the code — **every browser blocks audio autoplay** until somebody clicks something on the page. A page opened by itself on a screen at an event never gets that click.

Fix both in one request, in front of the class:

> The file runs locally on my machine. Fix two things.
> One: load images from a local folder named `images` next to the file, named `1.jpg`, `2.jpg` and so on, and music from a local folder named `music` following exactly the same pattern: `1.mp3`, `2.mp3` and so on. Do not use any internet address.
> Two: browsers block autoplay, so add an opening screen with a single "Start" button that starts both the music and the loop and goes full screen.
> If an image or a track is missing, skip it and carry on without stopping.

**Why numbers rather than song titles.** A page opened from disk **cannot read a directory listing** — it has no way to discover what is in the folder. `1.mp3`, `2.mp3` is a pattern it can guess and walk until it runs out, exactly like the images. Leave song titles in place and it will invent something like `background.mp3`, the file will not be found, and there is no error message — just silence. **Rename the music files to numbers before the session.**

Worth saying out loud while it works: "Notice why I numbered the files. It cannot *see* my folder — it only sees what I told it. The numbering is how I let it find them without reading out every name."

### What to say

> "Notice what happened here. The tool built something that works for it and does not work for me. That is not a bug — that is the difference between showing something and handing it over. When somebody builds you something, always ask where it will run in the end."

### The two tests before the night

Show [slide 11](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html): what happens with **only two images**, and with **a very long caption**. Both take under a minute, and both break often enough to justify the minute.

### And the question that arrives by itself

**Whose photos are these, and who agreed to appear in them.** And now, with music, there is a second one: **whose music is it.** For a private event at home nobody cares. For an event at a business they do.

**Two minutes in the journal** — table 2.

## 78–90 minutes — what goes wrong, and closing

**Now** open the [disruption cards](../../instructor/samples/session-03-cards.html) on screen. Read two out and run *the plan we built today* against them out loud: what breaks, what you do instead, and who decides. Ask the room for the alternative before you give yours — and ask the event's owner first.

Then send the cards to the chat. Running their own plan against a card is the homework.

[Slide 12](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html): a fallback needs three things — what you do instead, who triggers it, and by when you have to decide. "We'll see what happens" is none of them.

[Slide 13](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) for the exit check:

> The tool saved me **[what]**. The assumption that surprised me most was **[what]**. The task that now has a name is **[what]**, and it belongs to **[who]** by **[when]**.

### The homework — say it out loud

"Before the next session, build **one of the three files** for your own event. Not three — one. **I will open by asking what did not work**, so notice where you got stuck."

### Quick rubric

| Evidence | Not yet | Ready |
|---|---|---|
| Assumptions | Got quantities | Assumption column filled, and somebody said whether it is right |
| Ownership | "Invitations need sending" | "Aunt, by 1 October", in a cell in the sheet |
| Handover | A run sheet | Somebody who was not in the room can run the evening from it |
| Changing facts | "The venue is open late" | A source and a date for every changing fact, or "assumption" |
| The page | "It looks good" | Downloaded, running locally off local files, music plays after a click |
| Fallback | "We'll see what happens" | What, who, and by when |

## Three traps — know them before the session

All three will show up. Two of them are an excellent lesson when you are ready, and an awkward stall when you are not.

**1. Table layout and direction come back wrong.** A table can come back aligned the wrong way, or with its columns in reverse order — this matters most in Hebrew and other right-to-left delivery, and it happens even when you ask explicitly, which is why the requests here say so. Asking reduces it, it does not eliminate it. **Open one such file tonight.** If it happens, it is an opportunity: "Look, it did not even check its own work. Fix the file so every table is aligned correctly and the columns run the right way."

**2. The browser blocks music.** Covered above. Do not pre-empt it in the first request — let it happen.

**3. Local files.** Also covered above, which is why the folder with `images` and `music` is required preparation and not a nicety.

## Parallel tool tracks

**Claude with file creation (recommended):** real Excel and Word files, and Artifacts for the display page. This is the track the session is written for.
**Claude without file creation:** ask for CSV instead of Excel and open it in Excel, and a formatted document in chat to paste into Word. Everything still runs, it is just less impressive.
**ChatGPT / Gemini:** both also produce Excel and Word. Same requests. The display page arrives as an HTML file to download rather than a live preview.
**No tool at the learners' end:** that is the default here. You run it, they fill in.

## Handling problems without losing the session

| Situation | Facilitator response |
|---|---|
| Nobody suggests an event in the chat | Offer one of your own and ask "who is in a similar position?" There always is somebody. |
| The chosen event is too thin | Keep it, ask the room for the numbers, and borrow from the planning sources if you still need to. |
| The chosen event is too private | "We will not do that one on a shared screen", and move to the next suggestion. No deliberating out loud. |
| A file comes back badly laid out | Trap 1. A lesson, not a fault — fix it in front of the class. |
| No images, or no music | Traps 2 and 3. The combined prompt in that section. |
| The tool will not produce a file at all | The no-file-creation track above. Do not spend more than a minute on it. |
| The tool invented hours, prices, or a source | Stop and point. It is the most valuable finding in the session. |
| Questions overrun | Good. That is why this session is 90 minutes. If a block gets squeezed, shorten the Word document. |
| Somebody opened the disruption cards early | Give them a different card. |
| Time runs out | Drop the third part of the Word document. Not the disruption card, and not the download. |

## Adapting for different learners

- **Remote:** the session is designed for it. One shared screen, one shared document.
- **No device:** fill the journal in on paper. No gap.
- **For anyone who wants to run it themselves:** great, but warn them the quota may run out. Have them pick one file.
- **For anyone ready to go further:** build the page so it also works from a phone screen, and actually test that.

## After the session

Write down three things: which of the tool's assumptions surprised the room most, which question came up more than once — a recurring question belongs on the slides — and **which event was picked and how the pick went.** This is the first time the session has run on a live pick, and what you learn about that step is worth more than all the other notes.

Keep the two things that came up in the homework discussion, to open the next session with.
