# Full session plan — Session 3: Make a Shared Plan Work

**Time:** 55–60 minutes
**Learning outcome:** Learners can tell a constraint from a preference, date the facts that change, build a plan where every row has an owner and a date, and add a fallback that can actually be triggered.
**Session artifact:** One shareable plan: goal and constraints, who decides, a task table with owners, facts with source and date, buffers, one fallback, and a recheck list.

This guide can be taught from directly. It is written so that a facilitator who did not build the course can run it. Do not require learners to open a paid account, and do not put real people's contact details into a chat.

## Before learners arrive

### Prepare the room and tools

1. Open one chat tool in the facilitator account. The default demonstration uses **ChatGPT**, but Claude or Gemini are equally suitable — this session uses no web research.
2. Open the [sample planning sources](../../instructor/samples/session-03-plan-sources.html). This is a stable substitute for live booking pages, and the conflicts the lesson depends on are already in it. If you prefer a real case, check in advance that it contains at least one conflict between two sources.
3. Open the [projection slides](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) in a separate tab.
4. Have the [brief](../../sessions/session-03-plan-real-life-together.md) and the [learning journal](../../../site/assets/downloads/applied-ai-mastery-personal-journal-en.docx) on screen or in print.
5. If you have a board or a free wall, prepare sticky notes. A plan you move by hand teaches buffers better than any table.

### What learners need

- One real thing they are organising with other people. Ask them to think of it beforehand, or offer the journal example — a 40-person family event.
- A device with one AI tool, **or** paper and a partner to watch the demonstration.
- One place to keep the plan.

### Two facilitator decisions before starting

**Shared example or individual cases.** If the group is small and knows each other, build one shared plan for the example event and then let everyone copy the method. If the group is mixed, better that each person works on their own case while the demonstration stays on the example event. Do not mix the two — a class attempting both finishes neither.

**When to reveal the disruption card.** The cards are at the bottom of the sample sources. Do not hand them out in advance. A plan built in the knowledge of what will break is not really being tested.

## Session map

| Time | What the facilitator does | What learners produce or identify |
|---:|---|---|
| 0–5 | Opening: what are you organising that needs someone else | One real case to work on |
| 5–15 | Separate constraint from preference, and settle who decides | Two columns and one name |
| 15–25 | Collect changing facts, with source and date | The difference between “I knew that” and a dated fact |
| 25–40 | The tool arranges the facts, the class finds the conflicts | A plan with owners, dates, and buffers |
| 40–50 | Disruption card and building a fallback | What happens, who triggers it, by when |
| 50–60 | Recheck list, exit check, reflection | A plan that can be handed to someone else |

## 0–5 minutes — Opening

Show [slide 1](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) and ask: “What are you organising right now that needs someone else to make it happen?” Collect three examples out loud.

### What to say

> “A good plan is not the one that looks tidiest. It is the one someone else can pick up and run without calling you to ask what you meant. That is the only test we will use today.”

## 5–15 minutes — Constraint against preference

This is the stage that decides whether the session works. Show [slide 2](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) and build the two columns in front of the class.

Stress the line that is easy to miss: **“Grandma needs step-free access” is a constraint. “Live music” is a preference.** People defend preferences as fiercely as they defend constraints, and the plan then locks around something that could have moved.

### What to say

> “Everything you mark as a constraint shrinks the space of options. So it is worth testing each line: what actually happens if this is not met? If the answer is ‘it would be less nice’, that is a preference.”

Then require one name: who decides when preferences collide. Not “we'll talk about it”, not “all of us”. A name.

### If the class argues about who decides

That is a good sign, not a problem. Say: “You do not have to agree on who is right. You have to agree on who settles it, and to do that before you start.” If there genuinely is no decider, that is itself a result — record it as a risk in the plan.

## 15–25 minutes — The facts that change

Show [slide 3](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html), then open the [sample sources](../../instructor/samples/session-03-plan-sources.html) and demonstrate recording one fact: what it says, which source, and the date that source was updated.

Ask learners to record three facts for their own case, with source and date. Anyone working on the example event takes them from the sources on screen.

### What to say

> “‘I knew that’ is not a source. Opening hours, prices, and cancellation terms are exactly the things that were true last year and are not true now. We do not remember them — we check them and write the date.”

## 25–40 minutes — The tool arranges, the class finds

Show the request on [slide 4](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) and run it with the facts from the sample sources. The request explicitly asks for two things beyond arranging: flag contradictions between the facts, and flag what is missing.

### Expected intermediate output

A schedule with owners, dates, and buffers, alongside a list of contradictions and missing details. A tidy plan with no owner on any row is not the result you want.

### The moment that matters: the three conflicts

Show [slide 5](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) only **after** the class has tried to find them:

- **The venue** is available until 22:00 with full vacation by 22:30, but the plan runs to 23:00.
- **Route 47** departs last at 22:40, so anyone travelling by bus has to leave before the event ends.
- **The caterer** closes numbers 10 days before, and printed invitations take 7 working days after design approval.

### What to say

> “Notice what happened here. The conflicts were in the facts from the very start. The tool did not create them and did not hide them — it arranged what we gave it. Anyone who did not ask it to flag contradictions would not have seen them.”

### If the output is weak

- **It invented a time or a price.** Stop and point at the line: “Where did that come from?” This is the best opportunity in the session to show why source and date matter.
- **It produced a schedule with no owners.** Repeat “give every row an owner and a date” and re-run.
- **It flagged no contradictions at all.** Ask: “Until when is the venue open, and until when does our event run?” Let the class find the first one, then ask the tool to check again.

## 40–50 minutes — The disruption card and the fallback

Now hand out one of the [disruption cards](../../instructor/samples/session-03-plan-sources.html), or show [slide 7](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) and pick one with the group.

Run the plan against the card and ask: what breaks first? What breaks because of that?

Show [slide 8](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) — a fallback needs three things: what happens instead, who triggers it, and by when it must be decided.

### What to say

> “‘We'll see what happens’ is not a fallback. It is postponing the decision to the moment it becomes more expensive. A buffer is not wasted time — it is where the plan absorbs reality.”

## 50–60 minutes — Recheck list, exit check, reflection

Show [slide 9](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) and ask for only two rows in the recheck list: what to check, when, against which source, and who checks.

Then show [slide 10](../../instructor/samples/session-03-slides.html) and ask each learner to complete:

> My plan breaks if **[the disruption]**. In that case **[who]** will do **[what]**, and the decision has to be made by **[when]**. Before I send anything to people, I will recheck **[one fact]**.

One written reflection question: “Which of your preferences was disguised as a constraint?”

### Quick assessment rubric

| Evidence | Not yet | Ready |
|---|---|---|
| Constraint vs preference | Everything is listed as a constraint | Both columns filled, with a named decider |
| Facts | “I knew that” or “I saw it once” | Every fact with a source and a check date |
| Task ownership | “Invitations need sending” | “Aunt sends invitations by 1 October” |
| Fallback | “We'll see what happens” | What happens, who triggers it, by when |

## Equivalent tool routes

**ChatGPT (default):** The request above in ordinary chat, with the facts pasted in.
**Claude:** The same facts and the same request. Output tends to be better organised as a table, which suits this session.
**Gemini:** The same facts and the same request. Do not use Deep Research — there is no research question here, only an arrangement of facts we already hold.
**No tool:** Sticky notes on a wall or a table on paper. This is arguably the best way to show buffers, because you move one note and see what gets pushed.

## Troubleshoot without losing the lesson

| Situation | Facilitator response |
|---|---|
| The tool invents times or prices | Stop and point at the line. Move it to “missing”, and show that an invention looks exactly like a fact. |
| A learner picks an enormous event | Narrow to one decision inside it: not “the wedding”, but “transport on the wedding evening”. |
| The class argues about preferences | Return to “who settles it”, and record the lack of a decider as a risk. |
| Someone wants to paste a real guest list into the chat | Stop. Use roles instead of names: “aunt”, “music owner”. |
| The plan comes out with no owners | Go row by row and add a name. A row without a name is a wish. |
| Time runs out | Drop the recheck list, not the disruption card. A plan not tested against a disruption has not been tested. |

## Differentiation

- **No device or account:** A printout of the sample sources and sticky notes. The whole session can be completed this way, and sometimes better.
- **For learners who need structure:** Give them the example event from the journal and ask them to change only the dates and the names.
- **For learners ready to extend:** Ask them to find the fourth conflict, which is not on slide 5. It sits between two sources: the caterer clears equipment within an hour of the event ending, and the hall requires full vacation by 22:30 — so if the event ends at 22:00, the caterer is still there at 23:00.
- **Language support:** The request may be written in the learner's strongest language. Require the table labels — owner, date, buffer, source — in the course language.

## After the session

Note which preference in the room turned out to be a false constraint, and keep it as an example for next time. If the group's plan broke somewhere surprising, keep that too — it is a new disruption card. Remind learners that today's artifact is only measured when someone else tries to use it.
