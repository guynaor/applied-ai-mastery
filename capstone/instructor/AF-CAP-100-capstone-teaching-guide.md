# Instructor Guide — Capstone: AquaNode Mini

**Recommended duration:** 3–5 hours across one or two sessions  
**Student role:** Lead Applied AI Engineer  
**Primary outcome:** The student integrates research, data, communication, operations, automation, and CAD into one evidence-bound recommendation that remains internally consistent after review or change.

## Before the capstone

Confirm the student has access to all capstone files and the relevant materials from Missions 1–7. Open the mission brief, evidence register, deliverable register, decision log, controlled-change exercise, scoring rubric, and instructor reference guide.

Decide whether the capstone will be completed in one intensive block or two sessions. For two sessions, end the first after the preliminary recommendation and evidence review; begin the second with the controlled change.

Do not guide the student toward “proceed.” A well-supported revise or stop decision can be stronger than an optimistic launch recommendation.

## Teaching objectives

The student should demonstrate that they can:

1. maintain traceability from evidence to claims and decisions;
2. distinguish facts, assumptions, derived values, and unresolved questions;
3. reconcile conflicts across documents and disciplines;
4. produce mutually consistent deliverables;
5. set boundaries on automation and engineering claims;
6. update affected work coherently when one input changes;
7. explain where human judgment remains essential.

## Suggested teaching structure

### Phase 1 — Mission briefing, 15 minutes

Review the required decision: proceed to a bounded engineering-validation pilot, revise the concept first, or stop for now. Ask the student to explain what each option means operationally.

Set the rule: every consequential claim must point to evidence, an explicit assumption, or an unresolved gap.

### Phase 2 — Evidence and uncertainty map, 35–50 minutes

The student reviews the evidence register and adds missing links, confidence, limitations, and decision relevance. Ask them to identify:

- strongest supporting evidence;
- strongest contradicting evidence;
- repeated or dependent evidence;
- non-comparable measurements;
- assumptions that affect more than one deliverable.

Checkpoint: the student should not begin the presentation or final recommendation before identifying the highest-impact uncertainties.

### Phase 3 — Preliminary recommendation, 30–45 minutes

Have the student write a provisional recommendation with conditions, not a polished final answer. Challenge it with questions:

- What would make this unsafe or unjustified?
- Which evidence is necessary versus merely helpful?
- What decision is reversible?
- What must happen before money, manufacturing, or external commitments?

### Phase 4 — Build the deliverables, 90–150 minutes

The student completes the research memo, workbook, presentation, operations plan, agent specification, CAD source, validation log, and reflection. Encourage work in dependency order rather than file order.

Use the deliverable register actively. For each artifact, require owner, status, evidence dependencies, assumptions, validation, and downstream effects.

Instructor role: conduct short design reviews rather than solving tasks. Ask for evidence, consistency, and validation criteria.

### Phase 5 — Cross-deliverable consistency review, 25–35 minutes

The student checks that names, dates, dimensions, costs, statuses, recommendation language, and assumptions agree across artifacts. Select three important claims and trace each through all affected documents.

A polished package with contradictions should not pass this phase.

### Phase 6 — Controlled change, 25–40 minutes

Introduce the supplied change or another approved change. Before editing, require an impact analysis identifying affected claims, files, calculations, and decisions.

The student updates only what the change logically affects, records the revision in the decision log, and verifies that no stale claim remains.

### Phase 7 — Final review and defense, 20–30 minutes

The student gives a ten-minute decision briefing, followed by questions. Use realistic stakeholder perspectives: engineering, procurement, operations, customer success, and leadership.

End by asking: “What did AI accelerate, what did it fail to verify, and what decision still belongs to a human?”

## Instructor checkpoints

Do not allow the capstone to progress without these checks:

1. **Evidence checkpoint:** major claims have traceable support or explicit uncertainty.
2. **Data checkpoint:** transformations preserve source evidence and unresolved conflicts.
3. **Communication checkpoint:** the presentation does not overstate the memo.
4. **Automation checkpoint:** consequential actions require human approval.
5. **CAD checkpoint:** rendering is not described as physical validation.
6. **Consistency checkpoint:** all deliverables use the same current assumptions and recommendation.
7. **Change checkpoint:** the student performs impact analysis before editing.

## Likely difficulties and interventions

**Difficulty:** Starting with the presentation because it feels concrete.  
**Intervention:** Require the evidence register and provisional recommendation first.

**Difficulty:** Treating each deliverable as a separate assignment.  
**Intervention:** Ask which shared assumptions and decisions connect them.

**Difficulty:** Updating one file after the controlled change.  
**Intervention:** Use the deliverable register to trace downstream dependencies.

**Difficulty:** Hiding uncertainty to make the final package look complete.  
**Intervention:** Reward bounded recommendations and explicit validation gates.

**Difficulty:** Allowing AI to create unsupported technical detail.  
**Intervention:** Ask for the source, derivation, or verification method for every consequential detail.

## Assessment conversation

Use the rubric, but also ask the student to defend three choices:

- one choice that improved safety or reliability;
- one choice that preserved uncertainty instead of guessing;
- one choice where they rejected or corrected an AI suggestion.

A strong submission is not the one with the most content. It is the one with the clearest decision logic, traceability, verification, and internal consistency.

## Minimum acceptable evidence of mastery

The student produces a bounded recommendation, complete evidence and decision records, coherent deliverables across all seven disciplines, explicit validation gaps, safe automation boundaries, a parameterized CAD artifact, and a controlled-change update with no known stale dependencies.

## Extension options

- Conduct a second change request that affects a different discipline.
- Ask another reviewer to challenge the recommendation using only the evidence register.
- Require a one-page executive summary and compare it with the six-slide deck.
- Add a post-pilot decision gate with measurable acceptance criteria.

## Fallback plan

If a tool is unavailable, substitute a specification, storyboard, or validation plan rather than skipping the discipline. The student must still demonstrate the reasoning, dependencies, and verification criteria that the missing tool would have supported.
