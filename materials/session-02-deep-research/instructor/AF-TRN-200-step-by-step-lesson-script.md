# Step-by-Step Instructor Script — Mission 2: Deep Research and Information Synthesis

**Duration:** 120–140 minutes  
**Student role:** AI Research Analyst  
**Instructor goal:** Teach the student to compare unequal sources without pretending they are directly comparable, trace claims to evidence, and write a bounded decision memo.

## Essential definitions

- **Primary source:** Evidence produced by the organization or test that generated the underlying information.
- **Secondary source:** A source that summarizes, interprets, or repeats another source.
- **Independent evidence:** Evidence that does not merely repeat the same underlying claim.
- **Dependent evidence:** Evidence derived from, copied from, or relying on another source.
- **Comparability:** The degree to which two measurements were produced under sufficiently similar conditions to support direct comparison.
- **Scope exclusion:** A cost, test condition, material, service, or assumption not included in a quoted number.
- **Evidence matrix:** A structured table linking claims to sources, strength, limits, and decision relevance.
- **Bounded recommendation:** A recommendation whose conditions, uncertainties, and next validation steps are explicit.

## Before the student arrives

1. Open the decision brief, all four sources, evidence matrix, memo template, answer key, and rubric.
2. Confirm that the student can open all source files side by side.
3. Privately identify which claims are repeated, which numbers have different scopes, and which measurements are non-comparable.
4. Do not tell the student which design option to choose.

## Opening script — 0 to 10 minutes

Say:

> “Research quality is not measured by how many sources you collect. It is measured by whether you understand what each source actually supports, what it excludes, and whether two pieces of evidence are truly independent.”

Ask:

- “Can four documents contain only two independent claims?”
- “When is a numerical comparison misleading even if both numbers are accurate?”

Expected ideas: repeated claims do not become stronger merely through repetition; numbers can differ in test method, included costs, time period, geometry, or conditions.

## Read the decision before the sources — 10 to 20 minutes

1. Have the student read the decision brief.
2. Ask them to restate the decision in one sentence.
3. Ask what criteria would matter to that decision.
4. Write the criteria visibly before reading source conclusions.

Good criteria may include cost scope, structural suitability, corrosion, assembly, dynamic behavior, fabrication complexity, and validation gaps.

If the student immediately chooses a material, stop and ask: “Which evidence have you not read yet?”

## Source-by-source extraction — 20 to 50 minutes

For each source, repeat this sequence:

1. Identify the author and stakeholder interest.
2. Identify the source type.
3. Extract no more than five consequential claims.
4. Record the exact evidence supporting each claim.
5. Record exclusions, limitations, and unanswered questions.
6. Mark whether the source is independent or repeats another claim.

Instructor questions:

- “Who benefits if this claim is accepted?”
- “Was this measured, quoted, asserted, or inferred?”
- “What conditions produced the number?”
- “What is missing from the cost?”
- “Could this source be repeating another source?”

Do not allow “manufacturer source means false.” Teach that stakeholder interest affects scrutiny, not automatic rejection.

## Build the evidence matrix — 50 to 75 minutes

Have the student create one row per meaningful claim, not one row per document.

Required fields:

- claim;
- source;
- evidence type;
- independence;
- confidence;
- limitation;
- decision relevance;
- follow-up needed.

Demonstrate one example aloud:

> “The quoted price is evidence of a proposal under a stated scope. It is not automatically evidence of total installed cost.”

Checkpoint: repeated manufacturer claims appearing in a distributor document must not be counted as new independent evidence.

## Comparability test — 75 to 92 minutes

For every pair of numbers the student wants to compare, ask them to complete this sentence:

> “These values are comparable because they use the same or sufficiently similar ______.”

Possible fields: specimen geometry, load case, duration, environmental condition, currency date, included services, quantity, or measurement method.

If the sentence cannot be completed, require language such as “directionally informative but not directly comparable.”

## Draft the recommendation logic — 92 to 108 minutes

Before writing prose, require a five-part outline:

1. decision requested;
2. strongest evidence for each option;
3. strongest limitation or contradiction;
4. provisional recommendation;
5. validation required before commitment.

Ask:

- “What would change your recommendation?”
- “Which uncertainty has the highest decision impact?”
- “Is your recommendation reversible?”
- “What should not be inferred from the available evidence?”

## Write and review the memo — 108 to 130 minutes

The student completes the memo template. During review, inspect every consequential sentence and ask whether it is:

- directly supported;
- derived and reproducible;
- an explicit assumption;
- an unresolved question.

Require source labels or references close enough that another reader can audit the claim.

Do not accept false precision, combined cost totals with mismatched scope, or language that turns incomplete evidence into certainty.

## Debrief — final 10 minutes

Ask:

1. “Which source looked strongest at first but weakened after scope review?”
2. “Which repeated claim initially looked independent?”
3. “What evidence would most efficiently resolve the decision?”
4. “How did AI help, and where could it have hidden source dependence?”

## Minimum acceptable submission

- completed evidence matrix;
- explicit independence and comparability judgments;
- memo with traceable claims;
- bounded recommendation;
- clear list of missing evidence and next tests.

## Intervention guide

- **Student counts repeated claims separately:** Draw arrows showing the shared origin.
- **Student compares non-comparable numbers:** Require the comparability sentence.
- **Student treats all sources equally:** Ask about evidence type, method, and stakeholder interest.
- **Student refuses to recommend anything:** Explain that a conditional recommendation can be responsible.
- **Student writes before analyzing:** Return to the matrix and outline.

## Exit check

The student must define independent evidence, comparability, and bounded recommendation using examples from the exercise.
