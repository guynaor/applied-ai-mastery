# Step-by-Step Instructor Script — Mission 5: Constraint-Based Operations Planning

**Duration:** 120–140 minutes  
**Student role:** Operations Planner  
**Instructor goal:** Teach the student to construct a feasible, auditable itinerary from hard constraints, preferences, travel assumptions, costs, buffers, and required contingencies.

## Essential definitions

- **Hard constraint:** A requirement that must not be violated.
- **Preference:** A desirable condition that may be traded off.
- **Feasibility:** Whether all hard constraints can be satisfied simultaneously.
- **Optimization:** Improving a feasible plan according to an objective such as cost, fatigue, or value.
- **Buffer:** Reserved time or capacity that absorbs normal uncertainty.
- **Contingency:** A predefined alternate action triggered by a specific disruption.
- **Travel origin:** The location from which a travel-time estimate begins.
- **Fragile dependency:** A part of the plan whose failure affects several later activities.

## Preparation

1. Open the mission brief, constraint register, location candidates, itinerary template, calendar template, answer key, and rubric.
2. Confirm every travel estimate has a defined origin.
3. Mark the two recovery blocks as hard constraints.
4. Prepare the two required disruptions: a two-hour arrival delay and an unavailable planned site.
5. Do not build the itinerary for the student.

## Opening — 0 to 10 minutes

Say:

> “A plan is not good because it contains many activities. It is good because it remains feasible, understandable, and recoverable when reality changes.”

Ask: “What must be true before we optimize a schedule?” Expected answer: it must first be feasible.

## Classify constraints — 10 to 30 minutes

Review the constraint register row by row. Require the student to label each item hard or preferred and explain the consequence of violation.

Ask:

- “Can this be moved?”
- “Can it be dropped?”
- “Who or what makes it mandatory?”
- “Is recovery time optional?”

Correct any attempt to treat recovery as empty space. Explain that a hard constraint can represent health, safety, contractual, or operational necessity.

## Inspect candidate activities — 30 to 48 minutes

For each candidate, identify:

- location zone;
- opening window;
- duration;
- cost and exclusions;
- purpose;
- travel origin;
- information requiring live verification.

Have the student flag any estimate that lacks a clear starting point.

## Build the first feasible itinerary — 48 to 78 minutes

Direct the student to schedule in this order:

1. fixed arrival and departure;
2. fixed meetings;
3. required recovery blocks;
4. necessary travel;
5. safety and uncertainty buffers;
6. preferred activities.

Require exact start and end times.

At each transfer ask:

- “From where does this travel begin?”
- “Does arrival occur before closing?”
- “Is the buffer still present?”
- “What activity would be dropped first if time is lost?”

Do not permit simultaneous or overlapping commitments unless explicitly justified.

## Check budget and scope — 78 to 92 minutes

Have the student calculate the plan cost using only included amounts. Require notes for excluded or uncertain costs.

Ask: “Is this total a full-trip cost or only the supplied activity scope?”

Reject false completeness. A correct subtotal with stated exclusions is better than an invented total.

## Stress-test required contingency 1 — 92 to 105 minutes

Introduce a two-hour arrival delay.

Before editing, require the student to identify:

- directly affected activity;
- downstream dependencies;
- hard constraints that must remain;
- preferred item to drop or shorten;
- communication needed.

The revised plan must preserve all hard constraints. Ask the student to explain why each change was chosen.

## Stress-test required contingency 2 — 105 to 118 minutes

Declare one planned site unavailable.

Require the student to:

1. identify the purpose served by that site;
2. choose an alternate that serves the same purpose, or explicitly remove it;
3. recalculate travel, timing, and cost;
4. confirm hard constraints remain satisfied.

Optional disruptions may be added only after both required cases are completed.

## Create calendar-ready rows — 118 to 130 minutes

Check every row for:

- title;
- date;
- start and end time;
- location;
- notes;
- time zone;
- relevant contingency or verification note.

Explain that calendar export serializes a validated plan; it does not prove feasibility.

## Debrief — final 10 minutes

Ask:

1. “Which part of the itinerary is most fragile?”
2. “What early warning would trigger a contingency?”
3. “What is the difference between a buffer and a fallback?”
4. “Which preference was sacrificed first, and why?”

## Minimum acceptable submission

- complete constraint classification;
- feasible itinerary with explicit travel origins;
- realistic buffers and scoped costs;
- tested two-hour arrival-delay contingency;
- tested unavailable-site contingency;
- consistent calendar rows.

## Intervention guide

- **Student schedules preferences first:** Clear the schedule and rebuild from hard constraints.
- **Student omits travel origin:** Require “from where?” on every transfer.
- **Student removes recovery time:** Reclassify and explain consequences.
- **Student overpacks the day:** Require drop priority and buffers.
- **Student changes only the disrupted row:** Trace downstream dependencies.

## Exit check

The student must explain feasibility before optimization and distinguish hard constraints, buffers, and contingencies using examples from the itinerary.
