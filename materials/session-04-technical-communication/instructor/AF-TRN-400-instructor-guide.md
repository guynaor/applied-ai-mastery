# Instructor Guide — Mission 4: Technical Communication

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes  
**Student role:** Technical Communicator  
**Primary outcome:** The student can convert incomplete, cross-functional evidence into a concise decision presentation without inventing facts or hiding uncertainty.

## Before the lesson

Open the presentation brief, source notes, storyboard, critique checklist, answer key, and rubric. Prepare a slide tool, but do not begin with visual styling. The lesson should prioritize audience, decision, evidence hierarchy, and traceability before design.

## Teaching objectives

The student should be able to:

1. identify the decision the audience must make;
2. separate evidence, interpretation, and recommendation;
3. structure a six-slide narrative with one purpose per slide;
4. avoid unsupported cost, schedule, and performance claims;
5. use visuals to clarify rather than decorate.

## Lesson plan

### 0–12 min — Define audience and decision

Ask: “What should leadership be able to decide after six slides?” Require a one-sentence answer. Explain that a presentation is not a compressed report; it is a guided decision path.

### 12–28 min — Sort source material

Have the student classify each source note as fact, stakeholder concern, assumption, unresolved question, or recommendation. Discuss conflicts between departments and why disagreement should not be silently averaged away.

### 28–45 min — Build the story spine

Before opening slide software, require six slide headlines that form a complete argument when read alone. A useful sequence is context, evidence, problem, options, recommendation, next step.

Checkpoint: every headline should state a message, not merely a topic such as “Costs” or “Testing.”

### 45–65 min — Complete the storyboard

For each slide, specify purpose, evidence, visual, speaker point, and uncertainty note. Ask the student to justify every number and to identify what the visual makes easier to understand.

### 65–85 min — Create or critique the deck

The student builds a rough deck or critiques an existing draft. Emphasize readable hierarchy, limited text, consistent terminology, labelled assumptions, and source notes. Do not reward decorative polish that exceeds evidentiary quality.

### 85–98 min — Rehearse the decision conversation

Have the student present in six minutes. Interrupt with realistic leadership questions:

- What evidence supports that claim?
- What is excluded from the cost figure?
- What happens if the pilot fails?
- Which uncertainty matters most?

Require concise, honest answers.

### 98–105 min — Debrief

Ask which slide would change first if one source were corrected and why. This checks whether the student understands dependency and traceability.

## Likely difficulties and interventions

**Difficulty:** Turning every source note into a slide.  
**Intervention:** Return to the decision and remove content that does not change it.

**Difficulty:** Using vague topic headings.  
**Intervention:** Require the headline to state the slide’s conclusion.

**Difficulty:** Inventing unnamed cost exclusions or precision.  
**Intervention:** Ask for the exact source and scope of every figure.

**Difficulty:** Hiding uncertainty in speaker notes.  
**Intervention:** Put decision-critical uncertainty visibly on the slide.

## Discussion prompts

- When should a presentation show disagreement rather than resolve it?
- Can a visually weak slide still be analytically strong?
- What is the ethical difference between simplification and omission?
- How does the audience change the same technical evidence?

## Minimum acceptable evidence of learning

The student produces a coherent six-slide storyboard or deck with message headlines, traceable claims, visible uncertainty, a bounded recommendation, and a specific next decision.

## Extension

Ask the student to produce two versions of one slide: one for engineering reviewers and one for executives. Compare what changes and what must remain invariant.

## Fallback plan

If slide software is unavailable, use the storyboard as the final artifact and conduct the presentation from six paper or text cards.
